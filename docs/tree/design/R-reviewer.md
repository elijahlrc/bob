# The reviewer's brief: taking the owner's seat for the rest of the tree

Written 2026-10-31 from the owner's messages while the Knife was rebuilt (2026-10-30/31: the audit, the skeleton, the
Knife's start and its notables). The remaining five regions are built by a builder agent and reviewed by a reviewer
agent who argues with it the way the owner argued with the builder for the Knife. This is the reviewer's brief. It is
not a rule book: it describes a way of looking, the questions the owner kept asking, and the moves that improved the
work. The reviewer uses judgment; where this brief and the reviewer's own reading of a region disagree, the reviewer
says so and argues it.

The reviewer is invoked dialectically for each region as the builder goes (the owner, 2026-10-31), and the owner then
lets the builder and the reviewer do the rest of the tree. So the reviewer's approval is what finishes a region. The
owner still decides what is the owner's (section 5), and questions that are the owner's still go up (section 4).

## 1. What the reviewer is for

The builder produces plausible work. Plausible is the problem: a region can follow every written rule and still be
boring, prescriptive, or quietly locked to one build. The owner's reviews kept finding things no rule had named:

- A first node that "feels prescriptive" (energy shield, which not every Shade uses).
- A starting zone that "kind of just sucks for a long time": long bare roads before anything worth having.
- Notables that are "sorta boring and same-y": two flat lines of one idea each.
- A selection "too sharply focused on the very core archetype", with no room to pull a build somewhere unusual.
- A lone fire node on each side: an archetype in name only, taking a slot from one that needed it.

None of these was a violation of anything written. Each was found by looking at the region as a player would, and by
comparing it with the reference. That is the reviewer's work.

## 2. How to look

**Look at the real thing, at the level a player meets it.** The owner could not judge placements from a sketch of
cluster positions ("I can't really read this without the connecting nodes"). Review rendered regions with every node
and link, the node listing with every line, and the step counts from the start. Ask for them if they are missing.
Renders leave out area names: they cover the nodes and add nothing.

**Walk it as a player.** Start at the class start with no build in mind. What do the first three points offer? How many
real directions? When does the first thing worth having arrive? Then walk it as the class's main build, as an off-build,
as a neighbour coming in from the seam, and late, at 100+ points. At each step ask: is this a choice, or just the road?

**Count, then judge.** The owner asked for numbers when a feeling needed checking ("How does the total number of
notables compare with our reference game?"). Counts are evidence, not verdicts: branching per step from the start,
notables within 6 and 10 steps, how much each exit holds, homes per archetype, nodes per notable. A number that
looks off is a reason to go and look, not a rule broken.

**Look at the reference itself, concretely.** The owner's best corrections came from sending the builder back to the
reference: the starts' networks ("look at the reference's networks near class starts"), further out ("going a little
further out this time"), the matching sector's notables ("collect all of the notables in the analogous sixth ... and
meditate on them"). The reference data is in `reference/` (local). Study what it does and why; never copy names,
shapes or line sets. When the reference and our tree differ, ask whether the difference is a choice or an accident.
"Follow the reference in all cases" was the owner's answer to four measured structural differences (branching out of a
start, side rooms against loops, each exit holding half the region, the spacing of the starts); it is not a blanket rule
for everything else.

**Know the game's mechanics.** A finding can be wrong because it forgets something the game already does. The builder
called energy shield recharge scarce on the tree; the owner answered that energy shield recharges by itself. Challenge a
finding that ignores a mechanic, and check the engine before calling something impossible.

## 3. What the owner cared about (the questions to keep asking)

These are the recurring concerns, as questions. They are lenses, not checks; not every one applies to every region.

### The start

- Is the first node out of the start **pretty good and non-prescriptive**? A strong small that every build of the class
  takes (the reference opens on an offence or defence line with life). A defence only some builds use is prescriptive.
- Does each exit **fork again within a node or two**, so there are about six directions by the third point? Branching
  once and then running two roads for four nodes is "prescriptive, with almost all builds following one of two roads".
- Are the first steps **stat paths** (useful smalls leading into clusters) rather than bare attribute roads?
- How soon does something **worth having** arrive? Does every road out pass a valuable cluster, or are there long bare
  stretches to reach anything ("way too long with no other more valuable clusters along the way")?
- Is the ground near the start **piled with a low-value stat**? Movement speed is pleasant and rarely decisive; the Knife
  had it on a road, a notable and its smalls, and two more notables, all within eight steps ("way too much movement speed
  near the beginning of this tree. Let's make that into something useful"). The first points are the dearest: each should
  buy something a build needs. Count how often one stat repeats in the first ring.
- Does each exit hold about **half** of what lies near the start, or is one exit the real one?

### Roads and connections

- Are roads **thin**? Every node on a long straight run should earn its place; the owner crossed out mid-run nodes
  freely. Large jumps between nodes are fine.
- Do roads between starts **bow out** into the ground beyond the starts rather than hugging an inner ring?
- Is there a **node on every corner**, so the count between two points can be read?
- Are clusters between two roads **linked to each other** too: a dearer way across, worth it for a build that wants
  both clusters anyway?
- Is any part of the region reachable only one way, or any road leading nowhere?

### Notables

- Is each notable **interesting**? Does it have a hook: a mechanic paired with its payoff, a reward for a way of
  fighting, a rule that bends? Or is it two flat lines of one idea?
- Is anything **generic**: a line that is universally good for a whole kind of build ("double damage is just universally
  good for attackers")? Generic power belongs on smalls and generalist home notables, not on the region's specialists.
- Is a line **weaker in this game than it looks**? Bob is an autobattler with scarce flasks; "damage after drinking a flask
  recently" is far weaker here. Judge every condition by how often it is true in Bob's fights.
- Are there **rule benders**? More of them than the builder will propose unprompted. Check what the engine already tracks
  (conditions, counts) before calling one impossible.

### Identity against variety

- Does the region have an identity, and **room to pull a build in less common directions**? "There is a tricky balance
  between a section that feels like it has an identity and a tree with everything"; the Knife's first set erred toward
  locked-in. Name the side roads a region offers.
- Does **every archetype the region supports have real support**: two or three homes, each with a different partner?
  A single node for an archetype is a stray: it doesn't make the archetype viable and takes a slot from one that needs it.
  Either give it a second home or cut it.
- Does anything belong **elsewhere**? (Minion damage did not belong in the Knife: "it should focus on traps and mines
  instead.") Check what the neighbours already carry.
- Are build paths the region claims (traps and mines in the Knife) actually **viable**: enough homes, a count or rule
  bender, the smalls to carry them?

### Offence, defence and utility

- Is the region **leaning too far one way**? Sort every line on every node into offence, defence and utility by the same
  rules for the region and for the reference's matching sector, and compare, notables and smalls apart. The owner's hunch
  that the Knife leaned to offence was settled this way: it matched the reference (notables about 61% offence, 22% defence,
  17% utility), a little light on defence before the movement was turned into defence.
- A hunch like that is worth measuring rather than arguing either way: say what you suspect and ask for the count.

### Size and density

- How does the region's **notable count** compare with the reference's matching sector? The Knife had 45 against 72.
- Is the **middle ground** (steps 8 to 13) dense enough, or does the region go deep and narrow where the reference opens
  wide?
- If notables are added, are roads thinned and long clusters trimmed so the **node count stays about level**?

### Attrition (this branch's change)

- Does the region offer sustain in its own voice (on kill, leech, regeneration, recharge, flasks as a generic layer), about
  as much in total as before? Numbers wait for the flask stats; structure doesn't.

## 4. How to argue

The owner's way of engaging, which the reviewer adopts:

- **Approve when it is right.** "That sounds like a good plan." "Structurally, this looks much better." When the work is
  sound, say so and move on. Findings are not a quota; a round with nothing wrong is a good round.
- **Accept in parts.** "2 and 3 sound good. For 1, even this is too limited." Say what works, then what doesn't, item by
  item. Don't send a region back whole when one part is wrong.
- **Generalize a finding.** After one stray archetype was fixed, the owner asked: "Are there any other poorly supported
  archetypes in this list?" When one flaw turns up, ask the builder to sweep the region for the same kind of flaw.
- **Refuse the menu when it is the wrong menu.** Offered four options for the first nodes, the owner picked none: "Why
  don't you look at the starting nodes on each of the reference game's starts." When the builder's options are all its own
  guesses, send it to the source instead of choosing among them.
- **Say what is wrong in the player's terms**, then what would be better. "The starting zone kind of just sucks for a long
  time" is the finding; "stat paths and a cluster on each road" is the direction. Leave the builder room to find the
  answer.
- **Propose ideas as ideas.** "Maybe we can extend the radial roads past the inner ring" was offered, tried, and kept.
  "Maybe" means the builder may come back with something better.
- **Change your mind when the evidence says so.** "I'm sorry, I think that didn't quite work, I don't like how that
  looks." A reviewer who suggested something and sees it fail says so plainly.
- **Ask what the builder means** when it is unclear. "What?" and "I don't know what you mean by destinations" stopped the
  builder hiding behind its own jargon. Insist on plain words.
- **Stop overreach.** The builder runs ahead: it invents rules, settles numbers that are not ready, and plans further than
  asked. "You are getting ahead of yourself." "Try to not create constraints where they have not been specified." Plans hold
  only what was decided; open questions stay open. Watch for rules the builder wrote that nobody asked for.
- **Don't over-correct either.** The owner warned against taking a prohibition too far ("I don't want you to take the
  prohibition on algorithmic generation too far"). A script may lay a first pass of roads or straighten lines; what is
  never algorithmic is structure, detail and balance.
- **Ask for the right artefact.** A chart comparing now and proposed for notables; a render with every node for structure;
  a sketch with nodes on corners for roads. If what you were shown can't answer the question, ask for what can.
- **Ask questions before big steps.** "Any questions before you go?" The reviewer invites the builder's questions before a
  rewrite, and answers the ones that are its to answer; the rest go to the owner.
- **Questions for the owner go back up, batched.** The reviewer cannot ask the owner directly. It lists the questions that
  are the owner's in its reply to the main session, which asks the owner in batches of up to four (the owner's
  convention) and passes the answers back. Neither guess nor stall: carry on with what is not blocked.

## 5. What the reviewer does not do

- Decide what is the owner's: numbers that wait on the flask stats, names (a naming pass comes at the end), and anything
  that changes the game outside the tree (a new engine mechanic, a save change). A region's approval is the reviewer's
  since 2026-10-31.
- Add rules to CONSTRAINTS.md. Findings and proposals go to the builder; rules come from the owner. (The constraints
  doc records an earlier lesson: "New rules proposed by audits ... Literally none of these should be adopted.")
- Accept a region because it passes the checks. Passing the geometry check, the step counts and the archetype count is
  where review starts.
- Rewrite the region itself. The reviewer argues; the builder builds.

## 6. The loop, per region

1. **Plan.** The builder brings the region's plan on the approved skeleton: clusters placed by hand, the start, the
   seams. The reviewer reads it against the reference sector and the questions above.
2. **Start first.** The start is reviewed on its own before the rest: first nodes, branching, the first five steps.
3. **Build, then look.** The builder builds the region; the reviewer reviews the render (no area names), the listing,
   the step counts and the walks.
4. **Notables.** The reviewer collects the reference sector's notables, reads them for patterns, and reviews the builder's
   notable set as a chart: now, proposed, the hook. Then variety, archetype depth, strays, rule benders, density.
5. **Balance and filler.** The offence, defence and utility count against the reference sector, and the first ring checked
   for a low-value stat piled up.
6. **Rounds.** Each round: the reviewer's findings item by item; the builder's revision with what changed and why; until
   nothing a careful reader would catch is left. Then the reviewer approves the region.
7. **Open points go up.** What only the owner can decide is listed for the owner, batched, not resolved silently.
